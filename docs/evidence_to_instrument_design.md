# From historical evidence to instrument design

Track A examines both immediate assessed learning and the historical
measurement system. The findings below generate questions for Track B; they do
not determine item retention or prove how redesigned items will perform.

| Historical evidence | What it may indicate | What it does not establish | Track B implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong PRE concentration at observed minimums | Many concepts may have been unfamiliar, restricting PRE variation and weakening item relationships | That an item is automatically good or bad, or that a theoretical floor has been reached | Design the blueprint and candidate items to represent knowledge at lower baseline levels; assess whether items distinguish among them |
| Large immediate PRE/POST increases | Substantial change in assessed knowledge after workshops | An isolated causal workshop effect, retention, or behaviour change | Consider reducing exact item repetition with matched, counterbalanced forms; consider delayed follow-up only in a future study |
| POST item relationships were generally stronger than PRE | Greater shared variation in responses after instruction; restricted PRE scores may partly explain weaker PRE relationships | A validated single dimension or a latent structure created by the workshop | Retain explicit content coverage and evaluate dimensionality using purpose-built items and new data |
| Anxiety structure differed when 2021/22 was excluded | Sensitivity to cohort composition, educational context, administration, scoring, or historical versions | Which factor caused the difference, or that the earlier cohort should be discarded | Document instrument versions, administration, and scoring for future cohorts; ask the workshop developer about historical changes |
| Historical open responses were scored against key concepts | The measure captures free recall/production but may depend partly on verbal expression and scoring decisions | That multiple choice is inherently superior | Choose response formats to match intended outcomes; standardised recognition items may complement rather than replace content coverage |
| Perceived knowledge exists only as an aggregate 0–8 count | Overall metacognitive judgement can be described | Item-level alignment between perceived knowledge and demonstrated performance | In a future instrument, record perceived judgement for each item before showing response options, if feasible |
| Some historical items showed weak item-rest relationships or low loading | Restricted range, distinct content, scoring, wording, cohort differences, or other measurement features may matter | That the item is poor or should be deleted | Diagnose the source of weak association against the blueprint before redesigning or changing content |
| Historical open responses are available | They may contain genuine misconceptions or partial understandings | That useful distractors can be selected without systematic coding and review | Consider coding a manageable sample later to generate candidate distractors, then test those distractors in pilots |
| POST structure was heterogeneous across workshops and sensitive to Anxiety cohort inclusion | The tests may cover multiple content areas; some historical summaries may be more coherent than others | That total scores are useless, or that a particular factor solution is definitive | Begin with educational objectives and a content blueprint; examine whether domain-level reporting is theoretically and empirically justified |

## Design principles that follow

1. **Define the intended outcome first.** Perceived knowledge, free recall,
   recognition, vignette application, and real-life behaviour are distinct.
   Select an assessment format for the question it is meant to answer.
2. **Begin from workshop objectives and content coverage.** A content-based
   educational test need not maximise internal consistency at the expense of
   representing important concepts.
3. **Diagnose before deleting.** Low item-rest correlation, low alpha-if-item-
   deleted change, or weak factor loading alone is not a deletion rule. High
   alpha does not prove validity, unidimensionality, or adequate coverage.
4. **Document future versions.** Record item set, scoring rules, administration,
   and form version so later cohort differences can be interpreted.
5. **Use advanced psychometrics only when warranted.** IRT, DIF, equating,
   anchors, or item banking are not current requirements. Consider them only
   after constructs and blueprints are defined, items have been piloted,
   dimensionality and sample size are adequate, and the research question calls
   for them.

Track B remains ongoing. Historical analyses can generate design hypotheses,
but only new items and new pilot data can establish how future items,
distractors, or forms perform.
